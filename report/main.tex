\section{Introduction}
Pairs trading bets that the price gap between two related stocks returns to its mean.
We model the gap
as an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck (OU) process, select pairs with augmented Dickey--Fuller (ADF)
and Engle--Granger tests, and compare four trading rules on US and Indian
stocks from 2010 to 2026. We ask whether these tests find real mean-reverting pairs, how
accurately the OU model can be estimated, and whether any rule beats a simple distance
rule after costs.

\section{Mathematical model}

\paragraph{Spread.} For a pair $(A,B)$ with log prices $a_t=\ln P^A_t$ and
$b_t=\ln P^B_t$, the spread is $X_t = a_t - \beta b_t - \mu$, where $\beta$ and $\mu$
come from an OLS regression on the formation window only.

\paragraph{Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process.} We model
\begin{equation}
  dX_t = \kappa(\theta - X_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t, \qquad \kappa>0
  \label{eq:ou}
\end{equation}
Applying It\^o's lemma to $e^{\kappa t}X_t$ removes the drift:
\begin{equation}
  X_t = \theta + (X_0-\theta)e^{-\kappa t} + \sigma\int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t-s)}\,dW_s ,
\end{equation}
so $X_t$ is Gaussian with mean $\theta+(X_0-\theta)e^{-\kappa t}$ and variance
$\sigma^2(1-e^{-2\kappa t})/(2\kappa)$. The stationary law is $N(\theta,\sigma^2/2\kappa)$
and a deviation halves in $\ln 2/\kappa$.

\paragraph{Exact discretisation and estimation.} With daily step $\Delta=1/252$,
\begin{equation}
  X_{t+\Delta} = a + bX_t + \varepsilon_t,\quad b=e^{-\kappa\Delta},\quad a=\theta(1-b),\quad
  \varepsilon_t\sim N\!\Big(0,\ \sigma^2\tfrac{1-e^{-2\kappa\Delta}}{2\kappa}\Big).
  \label{eq:ar1}
\end{equation}
OLS of $X_{t+\Delta}$ on $X_t$ is therefore the conditional maximum-likelihood
estimator, and
\begin{equation}
  \hat\kappa=-\frac{\ln\hat b}{\Delta},\qquad \hat\theta=\frac{\hat a}{1-\hat b},\qquad
  \hat\sigma=\hat s_\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{2\hat\kappa}{1-\hat b^{2}}},\qquad
  \sigma_{eq}=\frac{\hat s_\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-\hat b^{2}}},
\end{equation}
valid only when $0<\hat b<1$. In short samples $\hat\kappa$ is biased upward;
Section~\ref{sec:results} measures the bias for our window lengths.
A simple step-by-step approximation would only give $b\approx 1-\kappa\Delta$, while this formula is exact.

% OWNER: M4
\paragraph{ADF is a test of $\kappa=0$.} Subtracting $X_{t-1}$ from both sides of
\eqref{eq:ar1} gives $\Delta X_t = a + (b-1)X_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$, whose slope
$\gamma = b-1 = e^{-\kappa\Delta}-1$ is zero exactly when $\kappa=0$; we therefore test
$H_0:\gamma=0$ (a random walk, no mean reversion) against $H_1:\gamma<0$ ($\kappa>0$). The
augmented Dickey--Fuller (ADF) test adds past daily changes to this regression,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta X_t = a + \gamma X_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_i\,\Delta X_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t ,
\end{equation}
where the lagged differences $\Delta X_{t-i}$ soak up day-to-day correlation in the noise, so
that $\varepsilon_t$ is pure noise and the test on $\gamma$ stays valid. We pick the number of
lags $p\le p_{\max}$ with the smallest Akaike information criterion,
$\mathrm{AIC} = 2k - 2\ln\hat L$ ($k$ parameters, maximised likelihood $\hat L$): a lag stays
only if it improves the fit by more than its penalty of 2. The
test statistic is the usual $t$-statistic of $\hat\gamma$, but under $H_0$ the regressor
$X_{t-1}$ is itself a random walk, so it does not follow a Student-$t$ distribution; it
follows the left-skewed Dickey--Fuller distribution, a functional of Brownian motion, whose
5\% critical value with a constant is $-2.86$ instead of $-1.65$. How often the test detects
mean reversion depends on $\kappa T$, the number of half-lives the window contains, which
E2a measures.

% OWNER: M2
\paragraph{Engle--Granger cointegration.} Each log price is $I(1)$: its level wanders like
a random walk, but its daily changes are stationary. Two prices are \emph{cointegrated} if
some combination $a_t-\beta b_t$ is stationary, so they cannot drift apart for good. To test
this we regress $a_t=\mu+\beta b_t+u_t$ on the formation window and run the ADF test on the
residuals $\hat u_t$, our estimated spread. OLS picks $\hat\beta$ to make $\hat u_t$ as small
as possible, so $\hat u_t$ looks more stable than the true spread and even unrelated random
walks can seem to mean-revert (a \emph{spurious regression}); the critical value must
therefore be more negative, about $-3.34$ at 5\% instead of $-2.86$. Regressing $a$ on $b$
and $b$ on $a$ minimise different errors, so the two tests can disagree; as a pair's labels
are arbitrary, we keep it only if both directions reject at 5\%.

% OWNER: M4 (s-score and distance/z-score rules); M5 (Bertram, from "For the optimal bands")
\paragraph{Trading rules.} The s-score rule (S2) uses
$s_t=(X_t-\hat\theta)/\sigma_{eq}$: open long when $s<-1.25$, open short when $s>1.25$,
close a long when $s>-0.50$ and a short when $s<0.75$. For the optimal bands (S3),
take the centred process $dX=-\kappa X\,dt+\sigma\,dW$, entry $a$,
exit $m>a$ and round-trip cost $c$. Solving
$\tfrac12\sigma^2u''-\kappa x u'=-1$ for first-passage times gives the expected cycle
length and the expected return per unit time
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[T]=\frac{\pi}{\kappa}\Big[\erfi\Big(\frac{m\sqrt\kappa}{\sigma}\Big)-\erfi\Big(\frac{a\sqrt\kappa}{\sigma}\Big)\Big],
  \qquad \mu(a,m)=\frac{m-a-c}{\mathbb{E}[T]} .
\end{equation}
With symmetric bands $a=-m$ we maximise $\mu$ over $m$ numerically.
The two benchmarks use no OU model: the distance rule (S0) picks the pairs with the smallest
sum of squared differences of normalised prices, sets $\beta=1$, opens when their gap moves two
formation standard deviations from zero and closes when the prices cross; the cointegration
z-score rule (S1) standardises the Engle--Granger spread by its formation standard deviation,
$z_t=X_t/\hat\sigma_X$, opening at $|z|>2$ and closing at $|z|<0.5$.

% OWNER: M1
\paragraph{Position P\&L.} Holding one unit of spread means \$1 long $A$ and \$$\beta$
short $B$; the daily return on gross capital is $(r^A_t-\beta r^B_t)/(1+|\beta|)$,
minus costs on every trade.

\section{Data and methodology}
\paragraph{Data.} Daily adjusted closes from Yahoo Finance (retrieved 26 Oct 2026) for 56 US large caps in 7
sectors (Jan 2010--Sep 2026) and 26 NSE stocks in 5 sectors (Jan 2012--Sep 2026).
Pairs are formed only within a sector and a market (196 US candidate pairs per window).
The sample uses today's large caps, so it has survivorship bias.

\paragraph{Walk-forward protocol.} Pairs are selected on a 252-day formation window
and traded for the next 126 days with frozen parameters; the window then rolls by
126 days. Selection keeps Engle--Granger $p<0.05$ in both
directions, $\beta>0$, a half-life of 2--63 days and at least 12 mean crossings a
year, ranked by the Engle--Granger statistic, top $K=20$.
Signals at the close of day $t$ are executed at the close of $t+1$, open positions are
closed at the end of the window, and costs are 10\,bp (US) or 15\,bp (India) per leg
per transaction. Thresholds are fixed in advance and never tuned; design choices
were frozen on 2010--2021 and 2022--2026 is reported as an untouched test block.
Every input to a trade is known before it is placed (a unit test scrambles all prices after
a date and confirms no earlier return changes); re-estimating $\beta$ and the OU parameters on
the trading window instead would raise the s-score Sharpe ratio from about 0 to 3.6.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Returns are on committed capital ($K$ equal slots; idle slots earn 0). We report
annualised return, volatility, the Sharpe ratio with its standard error, maximum drawdown, hit rate
and market beta; whether an OU rule beats the distance rule is tested on the Sharpe difference with a
circular block bootstrap and Holm's correction for the three comparisons.

\paragraph{Implementation.} Everything is written in Python (NumPy, SciPy and statsmodels). Each formula above has a matching function in \texttt{src/} (e.g.\ \texttt{fit\_ou},
\texttt{adf\_test}, \texttt{engle\_granger\_both}, \texttt{bertram\_bands}), and every result in
Section~\ref{sec:results} is produced by a cell of \texttt{notebook.ipynb}.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
We test the idea in three steps: simulations where we know the right answer, a perfect made-up
pair, and real stocks in the US and India, changing the data or the assets along the way (the
amount of data, the speed of the pairs, the market). A pair's \emph{half-life} is the time its gap
takes to close half-way, a \emph{basis point} (bp) is 0.01\%, and the \emph{Sharpe ratio} is
return per unit of risk, which must be roughly twice its margin of error to count as more than
luck. Figures and tables are at the end of the section.

\paragraph{Can we measure and detect mean reversion?} Our model describes each pair by the speed
$\kappa$ at which its gap closes. Estimated from one year of data it is too high, by about 18\%
for a gap that halves in 7 days and 96\% for a slow pair (E1), so pairs look faster than they are.
The ADF test that picks pairs finds a gap that halves in 5 days almost every time and one that
halves in 10 days three times in four, but a 20-day gap only one time in three (E2a,
Figure~\ref{fig:sim}, left), and every pair we select has a half-life of 21 days or less. The selection test must also be stricter than plain ADF: on two unrelated random walks,
plain ADF critical values wrongly find a pair 17\% of the time, against 6\% for Engle--Granger (E2b). The
formula behind rule S3 is correct, but checking prices once a day makes trades 35--99\% slower
than it assumes (E3).

\paragraph{What would a perfect pair earn?} A made-up pair that follows our model exactly, run
200 times through the steps we use on real stocks, earns 12.7\% a year against 24.2\% in theory
(E4, Figure~\ref{fig:sim}, right). The biggest loss, 4.5 points, is picking the pair (the
selection test finds it in only 74\% of windows); daily prices with a one-day delay cost 3.3,
estimating the hedge ratio 2.3, other estimates 1.0 and six-monthly restarts 0.5.

\paragraph{What happens with real stocks?} We traded all four rules on 56 large US stocks from
2011 to 2026 at 10\,bp per trade on each stock (E5, Table~\ref{tab:e5}, upper panel,
Figure~\ref{fig:e5}). The best rule, the cointegration z-score S1, earned 0.33\% a year (Sharpe
ratio 0.19) and the distance rule S0 lost slightly, but with a margin of error of 0.25 no Sharpe
ratio is distinguishable from zero, and none depends on the market. The individual trades explain
this: before costs S1 earns about 44\,bp per trade, twice the 20\,bp a round trip costs, and
after costs the three rules built on cointegration still win about two trades in three. Trades
closed by the rule almost always make money; the losses come from the 28--45\% that never close
within six months, which lose about 3\% each and cancel the gains. They are common because many
selected pairs are not real: only about 8 of 196 candidate pairs pass the selection test per
window, no more than the up to 10 expected by luck alone.

\paragraph{Does it hold in India, and do pairs stay pairs?} On 26 stocks of India's National Stock
Exchange (2013--2026, 15\,bp; E8, Table~\ref{tab:e5}, lower panel) all four rules again gain
slightly, S1 best (Sharpe ratio 0.38), none significantly (margin of error 0.27), and only about 3
of 55 candidate pairs pass the selection test per window. A direct test (E9) agrees: over the
next six months only 9\% of the 259 selected US pairs still showed mean reversion, against about
half had they kept the speed they showed when selected (median half-life 7 days). That benchmark
is somewhat generous, since speeds are over-estimated, but the fair figure is still well above
9\%. Selected pairs therefore mostly do not keep their mean reversion, which fits selection
picking lucky pairs.

\section{Conclusion}
With one year of data the ADF test reliably finds only fast mean reversion, and Engle--Granger
passes about as many pairs as chance would, most of which stop mean-reverting soon after
selection. The OU model and the optimal-band formula are correct, but $\kappa$ is hard to
estimate and even a perfect pair earns only about half its theoretical return. Before costs the
rules catch real mean reversion (about two trades in three win), but after costs no rule earns a
significant return in the US or India: the weak link is pair selection, not the trading rules.
Limits are survivorship bias, a fixed hedge ratio within each window and daily closes; a larger
stock universe, more stable pair selection and a time-varying hedge ratio are natural next steps.
